EMA or SMA? The big moving-average debate came down to a coin toss

On 4,268 US shares from the start of 2021 to November 2026, the 50/200 crossing rule did better with the SMA in 52% of shares and with the EMA in 45%, and the kind that did better in the first half of the period did better again in the second half only 51% of the time.

Before you read: guess

Same rule, same 50 and 200 days, two kinds of average. In what share of the 4,268 US shares did the EMA version end with the better result?

50%

Two averages, one rule

A simple moving average (SMA) weighs the last 50 or 200 closing prices equally. An exponential one (EMA) gives more weight to the most recent prices, so it turns sooner. Market Lab ran the same rule with both: buy when the 50-day average crosses above the 200-day average, sell when it crosses back below, 0.20% cost on each buy and sell, starting invested on the first day. Both versions were tested over exactly the same days on 4,268 US shares listed today. Only trading from 1 January 2021 to 17 November 2026 was counted; older prices served only to start the averages. No order was placed.

Nearly the same days

On the typical share, the two versions were in the same position, both holding or both in cash, on 92% of the days.

Where both bought within 60 trading days of each other (9,763 times), the EMA version bought first in 69% of cases, and the SMA version bought a median of 7 trading days later. The median is the middle value when all are lined up. Sales were closer together: a median of 3 trading days.

A small edge to SMA for the typical share

The typical share lost 1.3% a year with EMA and 0.6% with SMA, against a gain of 0.8% for simply holding. EMA ended ahead of holding in 1,816 shares (43%), SMA in 1,958 (46%).

The trades looked different. SMA made 16,197 trades, 40% of them winners, with a median trade of −4.4%. EMA made 16,074, 35% winners, median −6.5%. The mean trade was 6.4% with SMA and 5.9% with EMA.

Did the better one stay better?

Market Lab split each share's period into two halves of equal length, for most shares at December 2023, and tested each half on its own. In 3,296 shares, one version did better in each half. The same version did better in both halves in 1,693 of them, 51%.

Even where the first half's gap was 2 points a year or more (2,341 shares), the same version did better again in the second half in 1,182, 50.5%.

  • Thai shares: SMA did better in 312 of 570 (55%), EMA in 240 (42%). The same version did better in both halves in 116 of 196 shares (59%), a small sample.
  • US ETFs: SMA did better in 1,007 of 2,185 (46%), EMA in 956 (44%), 222 tied. The same version did better in both halves in only 463 of 1,174 funds (39%). In 510 funds, SMA did better in the first half and EMA in the second.

The limits

  • Missing losers. Yahoo removes companies that went bankrupt or were delisted. Only today's survivors were tested.
  • Hypothetical. No order was placed. Real trading meets spreads, taxes and orders that do not fill.
  • Halves are short. Each half covers about three years, is tested on its own and starts invested again, so its results are noisy.
  • A short period. Under six years, and nothing was held back for a later check.
  • Many rules, many symbols. This article compares two rules on thousands of shares. Some differences arise by luck alone.
  • Faulty prices left out. 103 symbols with jumps that look like unadjusted splits were dropped.

Nothing here is a forecast or a suggestion to buy or sell.
