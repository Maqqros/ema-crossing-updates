# Eight times in ten, the rule bought back higher than it sold

Date: 2026-11-21

On 3,273 US shares before 2022, the EMA 50/200 rule sold and later bought back 27,112 times, and in 82% of them it paid more to get back in than it had received when it sold, which explains most of why it fell behind simply holding.

## Where a gap comes from

Article 001 found that the EMA 50/200 rule ended behind simply holding in most shares. This one asks why.

The rule buys when the 50-day EMA crosses above the 200-day EMA and sells when it crosses back below, paying 0.20% on each buy and sell. Market Lab split the difference between the rule and holding into exact parts. Each time the rule sold and later bought back, it either bought back higher, which is growth it missed, or lower, which is a fall it avoided. The other parts are price moves after its last sale and trading costs. In this run the rule bought on the first day, like holding, so a late start plays no part.

## The price usually dipped, then rose past the sale price

On 3,273 US shares listed today, up to the end of 2021, the rule sold and later bought back 27,112 times. In 87% of these spells in cash (23,556), the price fell below the sale price at some point. The **median** lowest point, meaning the middle one when all are lined up, was 8.1% below the sale price.

But the rule only buys back once the 50-day average climbs back above the 200-day average, and by then the price had usually recovered. It bought back higher than it sold in 82% of the spells (22,207). The median buy-back price was 9.4% above the sale price and 24.9% above the lowest close in between.

## Short spells were nearly always dearer

How long the rule waited made the difference:

- Spells in cash of up to 120 days: 13,565, and 99.8% ended with buying back higher.
- Spells of more than 250 days: 7,451, and only 39% ended higher.

The long spells covered the deep, slow declines the rule is meant to sidestep. The short ones were false alarms, and there were more of them.

## Adding up the gap

Over all 3,273 shares, the parts were added on a logarithmic scale, which lets them sum exactly. If the whole shortfall against holding is counted as 100:

- buying back higher added 230,
- falls avoided by buying back lower took away 101,
- price moves after the rule's last sale took away 35,
- trading costs added 7.

The typical share had 7 trades, and costs took about 2.8% over its whole period. The rule fell behind mainly because getting back in cost more than getting out returned. In 68% of the US shares (2,218 of 3,273), the dearer buy-backs outweighed the avoided falls.

## Two examples

Coca-Cola (KO), 1970 to 2021: the rule sold and bought back 34 times, 31 of them higher. Holding grew 350-fold, the rule 66-fold.

The Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ, 1999 to 2021, went the other way. The rule bought back higher in 7 of 9 spells, but one spell, from October 2000 to May 2003, bought back 65% lower than it sold. The rule ended 9.6-fold against 5.1-fold for holding. Had it bought back at its sale price that time, it would have ended behind.

## Thai shares and US funds

Thai shares, 1990 to 2021: 1,190 spells, 75% bought back higher (897). US ETFs, 1994 to 2021: 4,851 spells, 84% higher (4,063). In both, nearly every spell of up to 120 days ended higher.

## The limits

- **Missing losers.** Yahoo removes companies that went bankrupt or were delisted. Many fell hard before they vanished, which is where selling helps most, so the missing data may hide falls the rule would have avoided.
- **Hypothetical.** No order was placed. Real trading meets spreads, taxes and orders that do not fill.
- **One way of adding up.** The parts sum exactly only on a logarithmic scale. Measured another way, their sizes would differ.
- **Chosen examples.** The five example symbols were picked because they are well known, not at random.
- **Many rules, many symbols.** Some results look good by luck.
- **2022 onward was held back** and has not been evaluated.

Nothing here is a forecast or a suggestion to buy or sell.
